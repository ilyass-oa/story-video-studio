# 01 · Story & script

Output: `episodes/<slug>/01-script/brief.md` (fill the template) and `01-script/script.txt` — the words
the narrator says, plus optional `[performance tags]` (skill 02). No headings, stage directions, emojis,
hashtags.

Before writing: read `history/STORIES.md`. Never reuse a story, source or hook from its table; a fresh
angle on a used source needs the user's OK. Keep `title:` and `source:` in the brief accurate — they go
into the history log. True stories: verify facts, list sources in the brief, never invent to dramatise.

## Length
45–60 s is the sweet spot: **120–160 spoken words** (the pace targets are 145–170 wpm). Longer only if
every extra line earns its place.

## Shape — built for retention
1. **Hook (first sentence, ≤ 8 words)** — a consequence, a contradiction or a forbidden fact that opens a
   question the viewer needs answered. "He committed the perfect murder." / "The ships stopped coming in
   1962." Never a greeting, never context first.
2. **Setup (1–2 sentences)** — who and where, with ONE concrete sensory detail.
3. **Turn** — the moment everything changes.
4. **Escalation (2–4 beats)** — each beat raises the stakes or adds a new question. Put a micro-hook every
   5–8 seconds ("Then…", "But…", "On the eighth night…").
5. **Payoff** — the line that answers the hook's question in a way that re-frames it. Best: the last line
   loops into the first so people rewatch.

## Psychology that keeps people watching (use deliberately)
- **Open loops**: raise a question early, close it late. Never explain the mystery in the first half.
- **Specificity = belief**: numbers, names, textures ("forty years", "a film over it", "not a drop of blood").
- **Contrast**: calm sentence → shocking sentence. Long flowing line → two-word punch.
- **The viewer's body**: sound, touch, cold, darkness — sensory words pull them into the room.
- **Stakes that grow**: something is at risk, and it gets worse.
- **Restraint at the peak**: the most shocking line is usually the shortest.

## Writing for the ear, the voice and the edit
- Every sentence must **show something** the editor can put on screen: an object, a place, a person, an
  action. "He felt uneasy" gives nothing; "the old man's eye, pale blue, with a film over it" gives a shot.
- Rhythm through punctuation: commas make sentences flow (momentum), full stops land a point, `…` trails
  off, `—` is a breath. Don't chop everything into tiny fragments: it sounds robotic.
- One-word sentences ("Slowly.") become full-screen beats — use them for the 2–3 biggest moments only.
- Tags where the delivery must change (skill 02): `[whispers]`, `[serious]`, `[trembling]`, `[very fast]`…
  ~1 per 25 words, only at turns.
- Say numbers the way they must be spoken if it matters ("nineteen sixty-two"). Keep names pronounceable.
- Quotes are fine (keep the quote marks). No filler ("so", "basically", "in this video"), no CTA unless asked.

## Before moving on — self-check (be harsh)
- Would the first 6 words stop your own thumb?
- Is there an unanswered question at every point until the end?
- Does every sentence give a picture? Mark the ones that don't and rewrite them.
- Read it aloud in your head at a brisk storytelling pace: any sentence you stumble on gets rewritten.
- Word count in range? Then checkpoint A with the user.
